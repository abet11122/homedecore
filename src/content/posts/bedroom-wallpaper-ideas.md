Wallpaper is the fastest way to give a plain bedroom character, and the decision that people most often get wrong — usually by papering the wrong wall or choosing a pattern that overwhelms the room.

Here's the practical version, with the numbers you'll need.

## Which wall to paper

**The wall behind the bed.** This is the answer nine times out of ten.

It's the natural focal point, the bed frames it beautifully, and — importantly — it's the wall you don't look at while lying in bed. A busy pattern facing you as you try to sleep is a genuine mistake.

**Avoid papering:**

- The wall opposite the bed, unless the pattern is very quiet
- Walls broken up by doors, windows and radiators — you lose the pattern and waste paper
- All four walls in a small room, unless the pattern is subtle

**Worth considering:** papering the ceiling. Unexpected, dramatic, and in a bedroom you actually look at it.

## How much you need

Measure the wall width and height in metres.

**Standard roll:** 10.05m long, 0.53m wide — covers roughly 5 square metres, but you'll get less with pattern matching.

**Quick method:**

1. Wall width ÷ 0.53 = number of drops needed
2. Wall height + pattern repeat + 10cm trim = length per drop
3. 10.05 ÷ length per drop = drops per roll
4. Drops needed ÷ drops per roll = rolls to buy

**Always round up and buy one extra roll.** Batch numbers vary between print runs, and matching later is often impossible. The spare also covers future repairs.

**Pattern repeat matters enormously.** A large repeat can waste 30% of each roll. Check the repeat before calculating.

## Peel-and-stick vs traditional

**Peel-and-stick**

Good for: renters, first-timers, small areas, anyone who wants to change it in two years.

Reality: needs a very smooth, clean, non-textured wall. It lifts at seams over time, especially in humid rooms. Removes cleanly from most sound paint — but not always from freshly painted or low-quality surfaces.

**Traditional paste-the-wall**

Good for: a lasting result, better print quality, and a much wider choice of designs.

Reality: more work, but paste-the-wall (rather than paste-the-paper) is far more beginner-friendly than it used to be. Removal is harder.

For a feature wall you plan to keep, traditional is usually the better choice. For a rental or a child's room, peel-and-stick.

## Patterns that suit small bedrooms

**Vertical patterns** — stripes, tall florals, vertical botanicals. Draw the eye up and make ceilings feel higher.

**Small-scale repeats** — ditsy florals, small geometrics. Read almost as texture from a distance.

**Textured or plain-effect papers** — grasscloth, linen-look, plaster-effect. Add depth with no visual busyness. Excellent in small rooms.

**Tonal patterns** — pattern in two shades of the same colour. Interest without noise.

**Murals** — a single large-scale scene, landscape or forest. Counterintuitively, these work well in small rooms because they create depth rather than repeating pattern.

**What to avoid in a small room:** large bold repeats in high contrast, and dark heavy patterns on all four walls.

## Styles that work in bedrooms

**Botanical and floral** — the most popular bedroom choice. Soft, calming, works with almost any furniture.

**Grasscloth** — natural woven texture, warm, no pattern to tire of. One of the most expensive-looking options.

**Plaster or limewash effect** — subtle movement and depth, reads as a finish rather than wallpaper. Very current.

**Toile and traditional prints** — best in a period home, and beautiful behind a simple bed.

**Geometric** — modern and graphic. Keep it low-contrast in a bedroom or it feels restless.

**Mural** — forest, mountains, a faded landscape. Dramatic and calming at the same time.

**Stripes** — timeless. Vertical for height, and they suit both traditional and modern rooms.

## Coordinating the rest of the room

Once one wall is patterned, everything else should quieten down.

- **Paint the other three walls** in a colour pulled *from* the wallpaper — usually its background tone
- **Bedding solid**, in one of the paper's colours
- **Curtains plain**, not a second pattern
- **One texture** repeated — wood, rattan, linen
- **Art:** keep the papered wall clear, or hang one piece only

A patterned wall plus patterned bedding plus patterned curtains is where bedrooms go wrong.

## Practical considerations

**Radiators and sockets** — papering around them is fiddly. Factor extra time.

**Sloped ceilings** — pattern matching on a slope is genuinely difficult. Use a plain or textured paper.

**Damp** — never paper over a damp wall. Fix the cause first.

**Wall condition** — fill and sand first. Wallpaper highlights bumps rather than hiding them, especially peel-and-stick.

**Lighting** — check a sample on the wall at different times of day. Patterns look completely different in morning light versus lamplight.

## Order samples, always

Order two or three samples and tape them to the wall for a week. Look at them in daylight, in the evening, and with the curtains closed.

A pattern that looks perfect on a screen frequently looks busy or dull on an actual wall, and a sample costs almost nothing compared to a room's worth of paper.

## Budget approach

- Paper one wall, not four
- Choose a small repeat to reduce waste
- Peel-and-stick for a first attempt
- Watch the pattern repeat number — a small repeat can halve your roll count
- Behind-the-bed wall only, since the headboard hides the trickiest bottom section

**Quick decisions:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Which wall? | Behind the bed |
| How many rolls? | Calculate, then add one |
| Small room? | Vertical, small repeat, or textured |
| Renting? | Peel-and-stick, on smooth walls only |
| Other walls? | Plain, in the paper's background tone |
| Before ordering? | Sample on the wall for a week |
