An attic bedroom is either the best room in the house or an awkward hot box, and the difference is mostly down to a handful of practical decisions made early.

Here's what matters, starting with the one that determines everything else.

## Headroom — check this first

Before planning anything, measure your ceiling heights.

**The general standard:** a habitable room should have at least **7 feet (84 inches)** of headroom over a reasonable proportion of the floor — often measured as at least half the floor area, or a set minimum width. Rules vary by country and region, so check your local building code before converting anything.

**Practical zones by height:**

| Height | Use for |
|---|---|
| Under 3 feet | Storage only — drawers, low cupboards |
| 3–5 feet | Bed, seating, low shelving |
| 5–6.5 feet | Wardrobe, desk (seated) |
| Over 6.5 feet | Walkways, standing areas |

Map your room by these bands before choosing any furniture. It makes every other decision obvious.

## Put the bed under the slope

The lowest part of the room is the least useful for standing and the most useful for lying down.

A bed tucked under a sloped ceiling feels cosy and nest-like — it's the defining charm of an attic room. Just leave **at least 30 inches** above the mattress where you sit up, or you'll hit your head every morning.

Position the head of the bed at the low end, feet toward the higher side.

## Built-in storage in the eaves

The triangular space where the roof meets the floor is unusable for anything else and perfect for storage.

- **Eaves drawers** on runners, pulling out into the room
- **Small doors** into the eaves for suitcase and seasonal storage
- **Built-in low cupboards** running the length of the wall
- **Open shelving** in the taller sections

Built-in eaves storage is the single best investment in an attic bedroom. It uses space that's otherwise wasted and it keeps the floor clear.

## Lighting a room with sloped ceilings

Standard ceiling pendants often don't work — they hang into headroom or sit at an odd angle on a slope.

**What works:**

- **Recessed spots** in the sloped ceiling, angled toward the walls
- **Wall-mounted sconces** at 60 inches on the vertical sections
- **Floor lamps** in the tall part of the room
- **Under-shelf or eaves-level strip lighting** — makes the low sections feel intentional
- **A pendant only in the highest section**, hung well above head height

Layered warm light matters even more here than usual, because sloped ceilings create shadows that a single source can't fill.

## Windows

Attic rooms often have limited natural light, and it makes a big difference.

**Roof windows** flood the room with light and are usually the best option. They also open for ventilation, which attics badly need.

**Dormer windows** add both light and standing headroom — more expensive, but they create a genuinely usable alcove.

**Gable-end window** — if the room has a vertical end wall, this is the easiest place for a window.

**Blackout is essential.** Roof windows face upward and let in far more early light than a vertical window. Fitted blackout blinds for roof windows are worth the cost.

## Temperature — the practical problem

Heat rises. An unconverted or poorly insulated attic is unbearable in summer and cold in winter.

- **Insulate between and over the rafters** — this is the single most important thing
- **Ventilation** — an opening roof window, or a small extractor
- **A ceiling or floor fan** for summer
- **Thermal blinds** on roof windows

Do the insulation properly before spending anything on decor. A beautiful attic room that's 30°C in July doesn't get used.

## Making it feel cosy rather than cramped

**Paint the slopes and walls the same colour.** This is the key move. Painting the walls one colour and the sloped ceiling another emphasises every awkward angle. One colour throughout makes the shape read as intentional and calm.

**Light, warm neutrals** for a small dark attic. Cream, warm white, oat.

**Or lean into it** — a deep colour on everything makes a small attic feel like a cocoon. This works surprisingly well in a bedroom, where you want it dark anyway.

**Exposed beams** — leave them if you have them. Whitewash if they feel heavy, leave natural for warmth.

## Furniture that suits the shape

- **Low furniture** — a low bed frame, low dresser, low shelving
- **Nothing tall against the sloped walls**
- **Built-ins wherever possible** — freestanding furniture rarely fits an irregular room well
- **A window seat** if there's a dormer

Measure carefully. Getting a wardrobe up an attic staircase is often impossible — flat-pack or built-in are usually the only options.

## Flooring

- **Carpet** — warm underfoot and helps with sound transfer to the rooms below
- **Engineered wood with a good underlay** if you prefer hard floors
- **Check the floor structure** before laying anything heavy; some attic floors are joisted for storage, not for a bedroom

Sound insulation between floors matters more in an attic than anywhere, because footsteps travel straight down into a bedroom below.

## The staircase

Often the tightest constraint. Check the headroom over the stairs and whether furniture can physically get up there before you buy anything.

A wall-mounted light on the stairs is a safety detail worth adding.

---

**Order to do things in:**

1. Check headroom and building regulations
2. Insulate and ventilate
3. Sort windows and blackout
4. Build eaves storage
5. Paint walls and slopes one colour
6. Bed under the slope, low furniture elsewhere
7. Layered warm lighting
