Most advice about small bedrooms is about tricking the eye. Some of that works. But the biggest gains come from layout — from clearing floor and creating one long sightline.

Here's both, starting with the measurements.

## The clearances you actually need

You can't cheat these, and knowing them tells you what will fit:

- **24 inches** of walkway beside a bed — the absolute minimum to walk past comfortably
- **36 inches** in front of a wardrobe to open doors and stand
- **30 inches** in front of a chest of drawers to pull a drawer out
- **30 inches** behind a desk chair to push back and stand

Bed sizes for reference:

| Bed | Size |
|---|---|
| Single | 38 x 75 inches |
| Small double | 48 x 75 inches |
| Double | 54 x 75 inches |
| Queen | 60 x 80 inches |

In a 9 x 10 foot room, a double bed leaves you about 30 inches on one side and 24 on the other. A queen leaves you almost nothing. Dropping one bed size frees more usable space than any decorating trick.

## Push the bed into a corner

The single biggest layout change in a small bedroom. A bed against two walls instead of floating with space on both sides can free up 20 square feet.

You lose easy access to one side — worth it in a child's or single person's room, less so for a couple.

## Create one long sightline

A room feels bigger when you can see across it uninterrupted. Look at the longest diagonal in the room — usually from the door to the opposite corner — and clear it.

Nothing tall in that line. No wardrobe blocking it, no chest of drawers halfway along.

This does more than any colour choice, and it costs nothing but rearranging.

## Go vertical with storage

Floor space is what you don't have. Wall space above 60 inches is almost always empty.

- **Wardrobe to the ceiling** — a full-height wardrobe holds far more and looks intentional; a short one with boxes on top looks cluttered
- **Floating shelves** above the bed, above the door, in the corners
- **A shelf running the full length of a wall** at 72 inches, above head height
- **Over-door storage**
- **Wall hooks** instead of a chair for clothes

## Choose furniture with legs

Furniture that sits directly on the floor blocks it visually. Furniture on legs lets you see floor underneath, and the room reads as larger.

This applies to the bed frame, the bedside table, the dresser — everything. A bed with visible legs makes a noticeable difference compared to a divan base.

## Floating bedside shelves

Bedside tables eat about 3 square feet each. A wall-mounted floating shelf at 26 inches does the same job with clear floor underneath.

In a really tight room, a single shelf plus a wall-mounted light frees up the entire footprint.

## Under-bed storage

The space under a bed is roughly 15 square feet of storage doing nothing. Use it:

- Drawers on castors
- Vacuum bags for out-of-season bedding
- A bed frame with built-in drawers
- An ottoman bed if you need serious capacity

Fill this and you may not need a chest of drawers at all — which is 6 square feet back.

## Colour and light

**Warm, light walls** reflect more light: cream, oat, warm white, a pale greige. Cool grey absorbs and flattens.

**Same colour on the ceiling** removes the contrast line and makes the room read taller.

**A mirror opposite the window** — reflecting light, not a blank wall.

**Layered lighting** rather than one overhead source. A room with light in three places feels larger than one with a single bright fixture, because the corners aren't dark.

## Curtains hung high and wide

Rod 4–6 inches below the ceiling. Extending 6–10 inches past each side of the window. Floor length, just kissing the floor.

This makes the window look larger and the wall taller. It's the cheapest transformation in a small bedroom and almost everyone gets it wrong.

## The rug rule

A rug that's too small makes a room feel smaller. It fragments the floor.

For a bed, the rug should extend at least 18 inches beyond the sides and foot. If that's not possible, run a large rug under the bottom two-thirds of the bed instead — or skip the rug and use two small runners at the sides.

## Fewer, larger things

Counterintuitive but reliable: one large piece of art makes a small room feel bigger. Six small frames make it feel busy.

Same with furniture, plants, cushions and objects. In a small room, fewer and larger always wins.

## What to remove

Small rooms improve more from subtraction than addition:

- The chair that holds clothes
- The second bedside table if only one person uses the bed
- Anything stored on top of the wardrobe
- Furniture you walk around rather than to
- Small decorative objects on every surface

## Doors and swings

If a door swings into the room and blocks usable space, consider rehanging it to swing the other way, or fitting a sliding or bifold door. Wardrobe doors especially — sliding doors reclaim the 36 inches a hinged door needs.

---

**The four changes with the biggest effect:**

1. Move the bed to a corner and clear the longest sightline
2. Take storage to the ceiling and under the bed
3. Rehang the curtains near the ceiling, floor-length
4. Remove one piece of furniture entirely

None of them require paint, and together they'll change how the room feels more than any colour scheme.
