Almost every living room that feels wrong has the same layout: every piece of furniture pushed flat against a wall, a large empty rectangle in the middle, and a rug that is too small floating somewhere near the coffee table. It is the arrangement people default to because it seems to maximise space, and it is precisely why the room does not work.

The room does not need more space in the middle. It needs the seating close enough for people to talk.

## The conversation distance

Two people can hold a comfortable conversation with **180 to 240cm** between them. Under 180cm feels close for anyone who is not family. Over 240cm and people raise their voices slightly, which is enough to make a room feel unwelcoming without anyone knowing why.

Measure across your seating right now — sofa front edge to the chair opposite. In most rooms with wall-hugging furniture it is well over 300cm, and the room feels cold as a result.

**The fix is to float the furniture.** Pull the sofa 20–30cm off the wall at minimum, and bring the opposing seat in. That small gap behind the sofa does something odd and reliable: it makes the room read as *larger*, because the eye registers space continuing behind the furniture rather than stopping at it.

> A room with everything against the walls looks like a waiting room, because waiting rooms are arranged for circulation rather than conversation. Yours should be arranged for conversation.

## The numbers to design around

**Seating and tables**

- **Sofa to coffee table:** 40–45cm. Close enough to reach a cup without standing, far enough for knees.
- **Coffee table height:** level with the sofa seat, or up to 5cm below. Higher looks wrong and is awkward to reach into.
- **Coffee table length:** roughly two-thirds the length of the sofa.
- **Side table height:** level with the arm of the chair it serves, ±5cm.
- **Seat to TV:** 1.5–2.5× the screen diagonal. A 55" screen wants roughly 2.1–3.5m.

**Circulation**

- **Main walking route through the room:** 90cm clear
- **Secondary routes** (between coffee table and sofa, around a chair): 60cm
- **In front of a fireplace:** 90cm clear, and nothing flammable within a metre

**Wall and hanging**

- **Art centre:** 145–150cm from the floor — gallery standard, and it works.
- **Art above a sofa:** the bottom edge 20–25cm above the sofa back; the piece should be roughly two-thirds the sofa's width.
- **Curtain pole:** as high as possible, ideally 10–15cm below the ceiling, and 15–25cm wider than the window each side so the curtains stack off the glass. This single change makes windows look substantially larger.

## Rug size — where most rooms go wrong

A rug that is too small is the most common and most damaging error in a living room. It shrinks the seating area visually and makes every piece of furniture look unrelated to every other.

Three correct approaches, in order of preference:

**1. All legs on.** Every piece of seating sits entirely on the rug, with at least 15–20cm of rug showing beyond. Needs a large rug — usually 240×340 for a normal room — and it is the most generous, expensive and best-looking option.

**2. Front legs on.** The front legs of every seat sit on the rug, the back legs on the floor. This is the sensible middle path and works in most rooms with a 200×290. Critically, *every* piece must follow the same rule — one chair floating off the rug breaks the whole arrangement.

**3. All legs off.** Only acceptable for a small rug used as a deliberate accent in a tiny room. In a normal room it looks like a mistake.

Leave **20–45cm of bare floor** between the rug edge and the walls. A rug that runs almost to the skirting reads as failed wall-to-wall carpet.

## Find the focal point, then commit

Every living room has one natural focus. Usually a fireplace, sometimes a window with a view, occasionally — reluctantly — the television.

Pick it, then aim the primary seating at it. Rooms feel unresolved when the sofa faces the fireplace and the chairs face the TV on another wall, because the arrangement is trying to serve two masters.

If the TV and the fireplace are on different walls, the honest answer is to choose. Angle the seating to favour one and accept the other is a secondary view, or put the TV above the fireplace if the height works — bottom of the screen no more than 120cm from the floor, or you will be craning.

## Three layouts that work

**The standard pairing.** Sofa facing a fireplace, two chairs at right angles forming a U, coffee table in the middle, everything on one large rug. Works in most rectangular rooms 350cm wide and up. Seats five, conversation distance around 200cm.

**The facing sofas.** Two sofas parallel, 200cm apart, coffee table between, console or fireplace at one end. Formal, excellent for conversation, hopeless for television. Needs a room at least 400cm wide.

**The L with a float.** Sofa along the longest wall but pulled 25cm off it, chaise or chair perpendicular forming an L, rug anchoring both. The best answer for narrow rooms, and the arrangement that most often rescues a room that "just does not work."

## Long narrow rooms

The special case, and the one that defeats most people. The instinct is to line both long walls with furniture, which creates a corridor.

Instead, **break the room into two zones across its width**:

- Main seating group at one end, arranged around its own rug
- A second function at the other end — a small dining table, a desk, a reading chair
- A console table, an open shelf or the back of the sofa marking the division

Two square-ish zones read far better than one long thin one. The zoning principles are much the same as in our [studio zoning guide](/post/studio-apartment-zoning/), just with fewer functions to fit.

## The test

When you think you are done, sit in every seat.

- Can you reach a surface to put a drink down without standing? If not, you need another side table.
- Can you talk to the person opposite without projecting?
- Is there a light within reach that you can turn on without crossing the room?
- Can you get from the door to your seat without turning sideways?

A room that fails any of those will quietly go unused, however good it looks in a photograph.
