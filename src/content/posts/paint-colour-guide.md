Almost every paint disaster comes from the same mistake: choosing the colour somewhere other than the room it is going in. A swatch under shop lighting, held against a white card, at eye level, in the middle of the day, tells you almost nothing about how that colour will behave on four walls in your north-facing living room in November.

Here is what actually determines how a colour reads, and a three-day sampling method that has never let us down.

## Undertone is the thing that ruins colours

Every neutral has a bias hiding under it. Two greys that look identical on the card can be violently different on a wall, because one is green-grey and the other is purple-grey.

The main undertone families in "neutral" paint:

- **Green-grey** — calm, slightly cool, plays well with wood and brass
- **Blue-grey** — crisp, can go cold and institutional in low light
- **Purple-grey (greige)** — soft and flattering, but goes pink under warm bulbs
- **Yellow-beige** — warm and forgiving, tips into cream and can look dated
- **Pink-beige** — very warm, difficult next to cool flooring

**How to find the undertone:** put the sample next to a sheet of plain printer paper. The paper reads as true white and the bias in the sample becomes obvious. Do this for three candidate colours side by side and the differences that were invisible on the card become impossible to miss.

The undertone matters most where the paint meets something you are not changing — the floor, the worktop, a fixed fireplace. A green-grey wall above an orange-toned oak floor will fight; a warm greige will not.

## Which way does the room face?

Orientation changes a colour more than the paint choice itself.

**North-facing** (south-facing in the southern hemisphere) — cool, blue-tinged, indirect light all day. Colours go greyer, flatter and colder than the card. Cool colours become *icy*. This is the orientation where people paint a room pale grey and end up with something that feels like a waiting room.

*Choose:* warm-based colours with some pigment in them. Warm off-whites, ochres, clay, deep greens, or lean into it and go genuinely dark — north rooms are excellent in strong colour and terrible in weak pastel.

**South-facing** — warm, bright, generous light for most of the day. Nearly everything works. Cool colours are lifted and made pleasant; warm colours can become almost too yellow at midday.

*Choose:* almost anything. If a colour is going to work anywhere it will work here.

**East-facing** — bright and slightly blue in the morning, then flat and grey from lunchtime.

*Choose:* colours you will mostly see in the morning. A bedroom works well east; a room you use in the evening needs a warmer colour to survive the afternoon flatness.

**West-facing** — dull and cool in the morning, warm and golden in the late afternoon.

*Choose:* with the evening in mind. Greens and warm neutrals are stunning in western afternoon light and perfectly fine at 9am.

> Test any colour at 9am, at 2pm and again at 9pm with the lamps on. A colour that looks right in only one of those three is the wrong colour.

## Sheen changes the colour

The same pigment at a different finish reads as a different shade, because sheen changes how much light bounces back.

- **Matt / flat** — deepest colour, hides wall imperfections, marks easily. Best for ceilings and low-traffic walls.
- **Eggshell** — slight sheen, much more washable, reads a touch lighter than matt. The sensible default for living rooms.
- **Satin** — noticeable sheen, very durable, shows every bump in the plaster. Woodwork.
- **Gloss** — maximum durability and maximum reflection. Doors, trim, and radiators.

A dark colour in matt absorbs light and recedes. The same dark colour in satin reflects and can look plasticky. If you are going dark, go matt.

## The three-day sample method

This is slower than everyone wants it to be, and it is the difference between one coat of the right colour and two coats of the wrong one.

**Do not paint patches directly on the wall.** The existing colour bleeds through, the edges bias your eye, and you will end up painting over patchwork.

Instead:

1. **Buy sample pots** of your three or four candidates.
2. **Paint A2 sheets of white card** — two full coats, edge to edge, letting each dry properly. White card gives you a true reading with no existing wall colour underneath.
3. **Leave a white border** of about 2cm around each, so you can see the colour against true white.
4. **Move them around the room** over three days. Tape one to each wall. Look at them in the morning, at midday, and at night under your actual lamps.
5. **Hold them against the fixed things** — the floor, the sofa, the curtains, the worktop. This is where undertone clashes appear.
6. **Look at them flat on the floor as well.** Light bounces off the floor onto your walls, and a card lying flat approximates what the lower half of the wall will do.

Then narrow to two, buy larger sample pots, and repeat with bigger cards for a day. Yes, this takes most of a week. Repainting a living room takes a weekend and £150.

## Practical rules that hold up

- **Go one shade lighter than you think** for large walls. Colour intensifies across a big area — a card's worth of pigment becomes a room's worth.
- **Ceiling:** the wall colour cut 25–50% with white reads better than default brilliant white in almost every room. Brilliant white ceilings above a coloured wall create a hard line that lowers the room.
- **Trim in the same colour as the walls**, in a different sheen, makes a small room feel bigger — the eye stops registering the boundaries.
- **Keep whole-house cohesion** by choosing colours from the same undertone family throughout. Rooms visible from one another should not swap between green-grey and pink-beige.
- **Buy from one brand per project.** Undertone consistency within a brand's range is far better than across brands.

## How much paint

For estimating: one litre covers roughly 12m² per coat. Wall area is (perimeter × height) minus windows and doors. Two coats is standard; three if you are going lighter over a dark colour, or using a strong red or yellow, which are the poorest-covering pigments.

Buy 10% more than the calculation. Touch-ups two years later never match a fresh batch.
