An island is the most expensive thing in most kitchen renovations and the easiest to get wrong, because the mistakes are dimensional and therefore permanent. A beautiful island with 85cm of walkway behind it will irritate you every single day, and no amount of styling fixes it.

So: measurements first, objects second.

## The clearances, in centimetres

These are the numbers to design around. They are not aspirational — below them, the kitchen genuinely does not work.

**Walkway around the island:**

- **100cm minimum** on any side you walk down
- **110–120cm** on the working side, between the island and the run with the hob or sink — you need room to turn with an open oven door
- **120cm+** if two people cook at once, or if a dishwasher and an oven door both open into the same aisle
- **90cm** is the absolute floor, and only on a side nobody works on

The commonest error is designing to 90cm all round because it fits. It fits until someone opens the dishwasher.

**Island size:**

- **Minimum useful:** 100cm × 60cm. Below this it is a table, not an island.
- **Comfortable:** 180–240cm × 90–100cm
- **Depth over 120cm** means you cannot reach the middle from either side — anything you put there is lost.

Check the room can take it: island width + 100cm + 110cm of aisle. A 240cm island needs a room at least 450cm wide. If your kitchen is under 370cm wide, you probably want a peninsula or a trolley rather than an island.

> If the numbers do not work, the honest answer is no island. A cramped island makes a kitchen worse than no island at all, and a good galley layout beats a bad island layout every time.

## Seating overhang

If people are going to sit at it, the worktop has to overhang far enough for knees.

- **Standard height (90cm worktop, 65cm stool):** 30cm overhang
- **Bar height (105cm worktop, 75cm stool):** 25cm overhang
- **Table height (75cm, dining chair):** 40cm overhang

**Width per seat: 60cm.** So a 180cm island seats three comfortably, or two with elbow room. Squeezing four onto 180cm means nobody can cut anything up.

An overhang beyond about 25cm on stone needs support — steel corbels or a hidden bracket set into the cabinetry. Unsupported granite or quartz will crack, usually when someone leans on the edge.

## Where the services go

Decide early, because these need routing through the floor.

**Sink in the island:** good for sociability — you face the room while washing up. Costs you the largest run of uninterrupted worktop, and you will look at dirty dishes from the sofa.

**Hob in the island:** dramatic, and needs extraction. A ceiling hood over an island is expensive and visually dominant; a downdraft extractor is neater and less effective. Also consider that spitting oil now has a 360° audience.

**Neither:** the best answer for most kitchens. An uninterrupted island is enormously useful for prep, homework, laying out food and everything else, and you keep the sink under the window where it belongs.

Whatever you choose, put **at least two double sockets** in the island ends. Everyone forgets, everyone regrets it.

## Overhead lighting

Pendants over an island are the single most-photographed and most-miscalculated element in a kitchen.

- **Height:** the bottom of the pendant sits **75–85cm above the worktop**. Higher and it stops lighting the surface; lower and you cannot see the person opposite.
- **How many:** two large ones, or three small ones, on an island up to 240cm. Even numbers of large fittings and odd numbers of small ones both look deliberate; three large fittings usually look crowded.
- **Spacing:** centre the group on the island, then space them so the outermost pendants sit at least 30cm in from the island ends.
- **Bulbs:** 2700–3000K, and get them dimmable. An island is a prep surface at 6pm and a wine table at 10pm.

## What goes on top

Now the styling — and the rule is restraint, because the island's value is its emptiness. A clear metre of worktop is worth more than any object you could put on it.

**The three-object rule:** at most three groupings on the island, occupying no more than a third of the surface, clustered towards one end.

A reliable trio:

1. **Something living** — a herb in a good pot, or a bowl of fruit. Real, and used.
2. **Something practical** — a wooden board leaning or lying flat, a stone mortar, a crock of utensils.
3. **Something low** — a shallow bowl, a stack of two linen napkins, a small dish of salt.

Cluster them at one end. The other two-thirds stays empty and available. That empty run is what makes a kitchen feel calm and expensive, and it is exactly what gets lost when the island becomes a landing strip for post and car keys.

### The material story

Keep the island's objects in the same material family as the rest of the kitchen. If your shelves are ceramic-and-wood, the island is ceramic-and-wood — the same logic we set out in our [open shelving guide](/post/kitchen-open-shelving-styling/). Mixing chrome, brass, plastic and stone on one surface reads as unresolved.

## Making it survive real life

The island is where everything lands. Two habits keep it clear:

- **Give the landing pile a home elsewhere** — a tray, a drawer or a hook by the door. The island cannot be the default surface if something else is closer to the entrance.
- **Reset it when you finish cooking**, not before you next cook. Wiping down while the oven cools takes ninety seconds; doing it the next morning takes ten minutes and a scraper.

If you have children, accept that a third of the island belongs to homework and craft between four and six. Design the seating end for that and keep the working end clear.

## When a trolley beats an island

If your kitchen cannot make the clearance numbers, a butcher's block on castors gives you most of the benefit:

- Extra prep surface where you need it
- Moves out of the way for a full clear floor
- A tenth of the cost
- No services, no structural work

Get one with a solid top, locking castors and a shelf underneath. It is not a compromise so much as a different, often better, answer for a room under 370cm wide.
