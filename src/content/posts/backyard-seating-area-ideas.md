A backyard seating area works or doesn't based on three things: whether it's big enough, whether the ground is right, and whether you can use it after dark.

Everything else is decoration. Here are the numbers.

## How much space you need

**Conversation set (4 chairs + coffee table):** 10 x 10 feet minimum, 12 x 12 comfortable.

**Dining set for 6:** 12 x 12 feet. The table itself is about 6 x 3, and you need 36 inches of pull-out clearance on every side.

**Two chairs and a side table:** 6 x 8 feet.

**Fire pit seating:** the pit plus a 7-foot radius of clear space around it.

**Distance between facing seats:** 7–10 feet for comfortable conversation. Further apart and people raise their voices; closer and it feels cramped.

## Get the base right

This is what separates a proper seating area from chairs sitting on grass.

**Gravel** — cheapest and easiest. Lay a weed membrane, edge it with timber or metal, add 2–3 inches of gravel. Drains well, looks good, and a DIY job.

**Pavers or flagstone** — more work, more permanent, best finish. Needs a compacted base of hardcore and sand, about 6 inches deep.

**Decking** — good on uneven ground or a slope. More expensive, needs maintenance.

**Grass** — free, but chair legs sink, it gets muddy, and you can't mow under furniture. Fine for occasional use, frustrating as a permanent spot.

**Size the base 24 inches larger** than the furniture footprint on all sides, so chairs stay on it when pushed back.

## Where to put it

Walk the garden at different times of day before deciding.

- **Where the afternoon and evening sun falls**, if you'll use it after work
- **Sheltered from the prevailing wind** — a windy seating area doesn't get used
- **Not directly against the house**, if you can — a destination further out makes the garden feel bigger and gives you a reason to walk into it
- **With something to look at** — a plant border, a tree, a view
- **With something behind you** — a fence, hedge or wall. Seating in open space feels exposed and people avoid it.

That last point is the most overlooked. Humans are more comfortable with their backs covered.

## Layout options

**Facing pairs** — two sofas or two pairs of chairs opposite each other. Best for conversation.

**L-shape** — a corner sofa or two benches at right angles. Uses a corner efficiently.

**Circle around a fire pit** — the most sociable layout.

**Built-in perimeter bench** — around a deck edge or against a wall. Seats a lot in very little space, and costs less than the equivalent furniture.

**Single retreat** — one good chair, a side table, a plant. Sometimes the best use of a small garden.

## Fire pit clearances

If you're adding one, these matter:

- **10 feet minimum** from the house, fences and overhanging trees — some areas require more
- **7 feet of clear space** around the pit for seating
- **A non-combustible base** — pavers, gravel or a fire pit pad, never directly on decking
- Check local rules on open fires. Many areas restrict or ban them, especially in dry seasons.

A fire pit is what makes a seating area usable for three seasons instead of one, but it's the part of a garden most likely to cause a real accident. Follow the manufacturer's clearances, not a blog's.

## Shade

**Umbrella** — cheapest and movable. A cantilever version doesn't put a pole through the middle of the table.

**Pergola** — a permanent frame, plants growing over it. The best-looking option and it defines the space architecturally.

**Shade sail** — cheap, modern, needs three or four solid fixing points.

**A tree** — the best shade there is, if you have one or can plant one.

## Lighting

The thing that decides whether you use the space after 8pm.

- **Solar string lights** overhead — zigzagged between posts, a fence and the house, or across a pergola
- **Solar path lights** leading to the seating area
- **Lanterns** on the ground and on tables
- **Uplighting** on a tree or a wall — makes a garden look considerably more expensive
- **A fire pit** as light as well as heat

Warm white. String lights overhead are the single biggest atmosphere change available.

## Privacy and enclosure

- Tall grasses or bamboo in planters
- A trellis with a climbing plant
- A hedge — slow but the best long-term answer
- A freestanding screen
- Outdoor curtains on a pergola

## Comfort details

- **Cushions** in weather-resistant fabric, with somewhere dry to store them
- **An outdoor rug** — defines the area and softens gravel or a hard base
- **A side table** within reach of every seat
- **A throw basket** for cool evenings
- **A small table** for drinks, even if it's a tree stump

## Storage

- A storage bench that's also seating
- A deck box for cushions
- A waterproof cover for the furniture

Cushions left out will be damp and mouldy within weeks. Plan for where they live.

## Plants around the seating

- Scented plants nearby — lavender, jasmine, rosemary
- Ornamental grasses for movement
- Pots at the corners of the seating area to frame it
- Something tall behind the seating for enclosure

Avoid anything that attracts wasps directly beside where people sit.

---

**A workable plan for a 12 x 12 foot area:**

Gravel base with timber edging, four chairs and a low table with 8 feet between facing seats, a fence or hedge behind, a cantilever umbrella, solar string lights overhead, two large planters framing the entrance, and a storage bench for cushions.

Doable over two weekends, and it's the part of the garden you'll actually use.
